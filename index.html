Spotting Butterflies

Data from the Global Biodiversity Information Facility (GBIF)

Somewhere in India right now, someone is crouched by a lantana bush with a phone, waiting for an orange-and-black butterfly to open its wings. When they share that photo, it becomes one of more than four lakh public records of butterflies in India.

Those records say a lot about butterflies. They say just as much about us: which species we notice, where we look, and when someone reminds us to go outside and count.

Part one

The butterflies India records most

Butterflies fall into a handful of families. The brush-foots are the big, showy group: tigers, crows, pansies. The blues are tiny and quick. Whites and yellows drift across fields, swallowtails carry long tails on their hindwings, and skippers dart about like moths.

In the flower below, each petal is a family, sized by how often it has been recorded in India. On each petal sit that family’s five most-recorded species, each drawn to the size of its record count. Tap one to meet it.

Part two

A year on the wing

Every line below is one species, showing how its records spread across the year. The one you picked is drawn bold. Almost every line leaps in September, which makes it hard to see anything else.

Switch to versus all butterflies to divide out that shared jump. A value above 1 means the species is recorded more that month than butterflies in general. The Plain Tiger, for example, stands out in April.

Part three

The September effect

The weeks after the monsoon are a rich time for butterflies across much of India. But September is also Big Butterfly Month, a nationwide citizen-science count first held in 2020. Every family peaks that month, and records arrive in a rush when people are asked to look.

The effect shows up across years too. Records of brush-footed butterflies more than doubled between 2019 and 2020, the year the count began, and have kept climbing since.

None of this makes the records less useful. It means they show where people look and when, as well as where butterflies fly. Most of the work is in telling those two apart.

Method

  • Records come from the GBIF occurrence API, filtered to country = India, pulled on 19 November 2026. Families: Papilionidae, Pieridae, Nymphalidae, Lycaenidae, Hesperiidae and Riodinidae.
  • Records are sightings, photographs and specimens shared to GBIF. They reflect observer effort as much as abundance, so easy-to-spot species near towns are over-represented.
  • Monthly totals are slightly below each species’ total because some records carry no month. The 2026 bar is a part year.
  • “Versus all butterflies” divides a species’ monthly share by the monthly share for all six families together.
  • English names were added by hand and should be checked against Butterflies of India before reuse.
  • Design inspired by Searching for Birds by Visual Cinnamon. Butterfly drawings are original.
